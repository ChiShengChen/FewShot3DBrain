Adding a new analysis task to a deployed 3D brain-MRI model usually means a few dozen annotated cases, no access to the data behind earlier tasks, and a backbone that cannot be retrained or copied per task. We compare eleven adaptation strategies -- LoRA, bottleneck adapters, bias-only tuning, linear probing, full fine-tuning, and EWC, LwF and replay -- on nine tasks from six public cohorts (glioma, stroke, hippocampus and white-matter-hyperintensity segmentation; brain age, sex and dementia on IXI and OASIS-1) at 32 labelled volumes each, under a subject-grouped whole-volume protocol with five seeds and per-case paired tests (393 runs). One property of the new task, knowable before training, decides the choice: whether it inherits a large task-specific head. Segmentation does, and training that decoder on the frozen encoder is at least as accurate as LoRA, which trails by at most 0.015 Dice while storing 88x less per task. The global tasks do not, and there only strategies that move the encoder representation learn at all; linear probing and bias-only tuning return a constant output although their frozen features rank subjects well above chance. A Swin UNETR replication reproduces the rule's shape, though there no strategy learns brain age under the unchanged recipe. Three further findings bear on practice: freezing shared weights is the only reliable route to zero forgetting, which EWC, LwF and replay do not deliver; the pretrained checkpoint is indistinguishable from random initialisation on two tasks and should be validated rather than assumed; and scoring random crops on the model-selection split inflates results enough to reverse conclusions. Code, splits and per-case predictions are released.
